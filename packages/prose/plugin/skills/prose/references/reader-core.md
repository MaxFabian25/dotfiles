# Reader core

Use this reference for clarity, organisation, or presentation work in English or Danish. Follow
the [authority and preservation rules](../SKILL.md#authority-and-preservation); language and
specialist references govern their own forms.

## Plain language in practice

Plain language makes relevant information findable, understandable, and usable for its intended
audience. A reader is a member of that audience. Write for the reader's knowledge, purpose, and
needed action or decision. When attributing a plain-language rule or checking its authority's scope,
use [source provenance](source-manifest.md) to locate the registered authority. For scientific or
quantitative explanations, identify material differences in reader knowledge, numeracy, or format
comprehension before choosing the explanation. Consult the [plain-science source
scope](source-evidence/plain-language.md#plain-language) when that designation matters.

- When you write for a reader other than the person asking, settle in one round of questions what
  the request leaves open: the reader and what they already know, whether they want a
  recommendation or a neutral comparison, the options really available, the criteria and features
  in scope, a length budget, and how the document will reach them.
- Lead with the main point and its support. Keep a limiting condition with its claim; put it first
  when the main point would otherwise mislead. Present a narrative sequence chronologically unless
  the requested analytical order serves the reader better.
- Give each paragraph a controlling idea. Split independent claims or conditions when their scope
  becomes hard to follow. Vary sentence length and retain a clear relationship between sentences;
  add a transition only when that relationship needs stating.
- Fix argument and organisation before polishing sentences and words. When a word substitution
  changes the claim, recast the sentence and check the evidence and caveat again.
- Prefer a direct verb and the shortest exact word. Name the technical object, state, or action;
  preserve an established term and explain it when readers may not know it. Use one designation
  consistently for one concept.
- Prefer active voice when responsibility matters. Passive voice can serve an unknown, irrelevant,
  or deliberately backgrounded actor. Keep modifiers and pronouns attached to their intended
  targets; check the scope of negation, conditions, and comparisons.
- Use familiar context to introduce new information. Compare like with like and name an unclear
  comparison basis. Match the strength of the claim to the evidence. Remove redundant hedges while
  keeping distinct conditions and limitations. State what an evidence gap prevents you from
  concluding.
- While researching for a deliverable, keep each factual claim with its source passage and link,
  or mark it as your inference. When the sources are silent, say the point is not stated; silence
  is not a *No*. Give a legal or regulatory conclusion in the authority's own words with its
  citation, or label it as your reading.
- Show an abstract financial or contractual mechanism through one worked example that follows one
  person with the supplied terms. Label any assumed figure as illustrative.
- Keep figurative language restrained in analytical claims and understandable across the intended
  audience. Preserve deliberate literary voice and logical or emphatic negation.
- Apply the [abbreviation rules](terminology-selection.md#abbreviations) before introducing an
  abbreviation and the [terminology rules](terminology-selection.md) when selecting controlled
  terms.
- Maintain one authoritative guide or procedure and link to it where readers need the shared
  material.

## Reasoning checks

At a suspected gap in reasoning, restate the claim without its rhythm or balanced contrast.
Then express the implied relationship as a cause, condition, contrast, or consequence. If either
step needs information the source lacks, query that information, qualify the claim, or remove
the unsupported inference. Retain the original rhetorical form when its meaning and support hold.

## Presentation choices

Use a specific heading that predicts its contents. In a divided document, use consecutive heading
levels for hierarchy and peer subsections for genuine divisions. A conventional academic heading
can identify a recognised function. Keep essential information in the main text; keep parentheses
brief and avoid nesting them. Use [Source text](source-text.md) for quoted, interpolated, or
translated wording and the governing convention for other bracketed forms.

Let content determine the number of list items. Use prose for one item or a connected line of
reasoning; use a list for a genuine set and a table for comparison across dimensions. State whether
items are requirements, alternatives, or examples. Number them when order, rank, count, or later
reference matters. Keep item syntax consistent, introduce the list's purpose, and nest only for a
real parent-child relationship. State whether a choice permits both alternatives or exactly one.
Answer in the body any question the gathered sources can answer, for each option compared, and keep
a list of next steps to actions only the reader can take.

Address the reader directly when useful. Use role names accurately and identify the author group
or recommending authority when it matters. Describe software, hardware, and services through
observable functions or states. Present disagreement by stating what a source addresses and where it falls
short.

Use capitals for governed forms rather than emphasis. Use bold for useful structure, labels, or
scan points whose following text adds information. Use italics sparingly and follow the controlling
convention for defined terms and words discussed as language. Format exact technical text as code.
Keep punctuation and inflectional endings outside emphasis unless they belong to the emphasised
material. Follow the locale or owning authority for personal names, addresses, dates, and suffixes.

Use humour, greetings, agreement, and other social wording when they serve the reader or occasion.
Keep instructions and consequential explanations clear.

## Remove wording that carries no content

Keep the supported fact or action. Remove redundant clauses, empty praise, invented attributions,
unneeded transitions, and summaries that add no information or useful social function. State a
positive point directly instead of building an unnecessary binary contrast. A claim that fits any
plausible subject needs specific evidence or removal. Summarise a governing provision in plain words
instead of copying its clauses, and give its exact wording only when the reader needs it, after the
summary. State each fact once in a summary. End on the last useful point.

Remove placeholders, leaked tool output, tracking parameters, and conversational leftovers from a
finished deliverable. Express a state in words rather than an emoji or status glyph. These are
textual choices, not evidence of who or what wrote a passage.

## Reader testing

When the task includes testing reader understanding, use the [reader-testing
process](publication-verification.md#reader-testing).

Before you hand over a document written for a reader other than the person asking, such as a
briefing, comparison, or assessment, have a fresh agent read only the document and a short
description of that reader. Ask it for passages that mislead, repeat a fact, leave a term
unexplained, or answer a question the reader did not ask, and resolve each one. When the writer is
a model whose maker the document assesses, also have a model from another maker check the factual
claims and look for framing that favours the writer's maker.

Complete the requested prose when its required information is clear, usable, and faithful to the
source and the selected rules.
