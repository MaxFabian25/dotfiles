# Inclusive language

Apply this branch when people, communities, personal or demographic characteristics,
accessibility terminology, or non-inclusive terms are present.

Apply these inclusive-language rules:

- Use the form of name and title that each named person uses. Use that person's pronouns. When a
  name, title, or pronoun is unclear and direct consultation is practical and in scope, ask the
  person. For a name with a prefix, such as *de* or *van*, follow the form the person uses in their
  publications or official records; otherwise, apply the [personal-name
  rule](reader-core.md#presentation-choices). Let the selected citation style control
  alphabetisation.
- Use the terms that communities use for themselves. If preferences differ within a community, do
  not present one term as universal.
- When examples depict people, vary relevant names, naming structures, genders, ages, body types,
  abilities, families, cultures, and locations. Vary occupations and economic circumstances when
  relevant.
- Do not imply that one ethnicity, gender, culture, family, ability, or income level is the default.
- When an example names several regions, use equivalent references. Compare countries with
  countries, states with states, and continents with continents. Verify disputed place names with
  the applicable authority.
- Do not generalise about people, countries, regions, or cultures, including with positive or
  neutral stereotypes.
- Keep each example truthful to its subject and purpose.
- Research the current usage and history of a non-inclusive term.
  Consider the audience and context before retaining or replacing it.
- Prefer a clearer alternative when credible feedback identifies harm or difficulty understanding
  the wording.
- Avoid ableist language, graphic or metaphorical language, and socially charged terms for
  technical concepts. Use the most precise and well-understood term for the context.
- Use *accessible* and *accessibility* only for things that are easy to use for everyone, including
  people with disabilities. Otherwise, name the specific characteristic that makes the thing easy
  to use.
- Use *assistive* only for devices, technologies, and organisations that help people with
  disabilities. In other contexts, name the item directly.
- Use a colour word only for an actual visual property or a defined technical colour term. Do not
  assign good, bad, safe, or unsafe meanings to colours.
- Do not use *male* or *female* to describe a connector. Use *plug* or *connector* instead of
  *male*. Use *socket*, *port*, or another term appropriate to the context instead of *female*. In
  user materials, describe the connector by its shape, size, icon, or another identifying feature.
  Preserve exact standard, product, interface, or source terminology. Explain unavoidable legacy
  wording outside the exact literal.
- Replace or write around a non-inclusive term when meaning
  remains clear.
- Prefer a direct description over a one-word replacement when it states the operation more
  clearly.
- If an established term is needed to prevent confusion, put it in parentheses after the preferred
  term on first use. Use only the preferred term thereafter.
- Preserve an unavoidable code item, interface label, legal designation, product name, or
  quotation exactly. Format it under its governing convention and explain it on first use.
- After that explanation, use the preferred term. Repeat the non-inclusive term only when readers
  must identify the code item or designation.
- Include personal or demographic characteristics only to identify subjects, support claims, explain differences, or complete requested actions.
- When a personal characteristic meets one of these conditions, describe it as specifically as the evidence and context justify.
- Use person-first or identity-first language according to the named person's or community's
  preference. Name people rather than turning a characteristic into a collective noun.
- Describe specific access or support needs supported by the person or source. A diagnosis or group
  label alone establishes neither a uniform experience nor an individual's ability.
- Do not describe one group as *normal* when comparing it with another. Name each comparison group precisely. This rule does not constrain a technical term such as *normal distribution*.
- Describe every relevant characteristic in neutral, factual terms. Avoid pity, patronising language,
  stereotypes, and value judgements. Describe suffering, victimhood, or confinement only when the
  source establishes it.
- Report beliefs, practices, observances, and preferences only when the person states them or a
  source establishes them.
- Avoid gendered terms where possible. Preserve a gendered term when an official title or
  designation requires it.
- Do not infer a person's gender, pronouns, or title from a name or appearance. If a required title
  is unknown, use the person's name alone when controlling style permits it.
- Do not assume binary gender categories in examples or generalisations unless the distinction is
  material and supported.
- Clarify a person's pronouns when they may be unclear to readers.
- Use singular *they* for a person whose gender is unknown or irrelevant and for a person who uses
  those pronouns. Use it in place of generic *he*, *she*, or alternating gendered pronouns.

## Terminology

*Bias-free language* is APA guidance for writing about people and groups.

| Scope | Preferred designation |
| --- | --- |
| Language that names the person before a characteristic or diagnosis | person-first language |
| Language that names an identity before the person noun | identity-first language |
| Terms that people use to identify themselves | self-identification |
| Gender-neutral third-person singular pronoun | singular they |
| Content, product, service, or environment that is easy to use for everyone, including people with disabilities | accessible |
| Device, technology, or organisation that helps people with disabilities | assistive |

Complete this branch when every reference to people or communities is accurate, relevant,
self-identified when known, and avoids unsupported generalisation.
